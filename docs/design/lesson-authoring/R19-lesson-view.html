Unit 4: The Lightning Thief

Lesson 4: Meeting a narrator who doesn’t feel normal

Investigation 1Core Reading50 min
Focus standards
Supporting standards
ELD standards
Lesson texts
The Lightning Thief, Rick Riordan. Novel, Chapter 1, pp. 1–8
Vocabulary
envy, frayed, probation, immortal
For additional support: recognize, expelled, threatened, spawn, detention, dyslexia
Materials
The novel; Unit Vocabulary graphic organizer; sticky notes for annotation

Student learning objectives

  1. Content. Students will cite evidence from the narrator’s opening comments (pp. 1–8) to infer what he fears and values, and predict his role in the story.
  2. Language. Students will use “This suggests that ...” to connect a detail from the text to an inference.
  3. Foundational skills. Students will use the root mort- to work out the meaning of immortal and related words.

Essential questions

  • How do myths reflect cultural values?
  • How do retellings change across cultures and generations?

Connections

Knowledge-building
Students begin the anchor novel by meeting a narrator who tells a myth-like life story in a modern voice.
Enduring understanding
The way narrators describe themselves shapes what readers expect from their stories.
Future lessons
Lesson 5 turns today’s inferences into an explanatory paragraph about Chapter 1.
Unit performance task
Identifying and explaining emerging themes.

1

Launch

5 min
Teacher
Have students sit with their reading partner. Write the word hero on the board.

Turn and Talk

Say these directions:
Turn to your partner. Partner A, describe a hero from a story you know. Partner B, add one thing that hero had to overcome. You have two minutes.
Ask:
What if the hero of a story was a kid who keeps getting kicked out of school? What might that story be about?
Exemplar response
I think it could be about a kid who people think is a problem, but he finds out he is special and has to prove it.
Say:
Today we meet a narrator like that. As we read, we’ll collect what he says about himself and decide what it suggests about him.
2

Literacy Lab

10 min
Teacher
This mini-lesson teaches one way to connect evidence to an inference. Students use it again in Part B.

Language Study

Say:
When I read a detail, I ask what it shows that the narrator didn’t say directly. Then I connect the two with “This suggests that.” Watch me try it with a sentence I wrote.
Display:
The narrator says he has been kicked out of six schools. This suggests that adults see him as a problem before they know him.
Ask:
Which part of the model is the evidence, and which part is the inference?
Exemplar response
The evidence is that he has been kicked out of six schools. The inference is that adults see him as a problem.
Ask:
Why does the writer use “This suggests that” instead of “This proves that”?
Exemplar response
Because it’s an idea based on clues. The book doesn’t say it straight out.

Learning in Action

30 min

Part A: Reading the opening with pause points RL.6.1L.6.418 min

Teacher tip
The narrator mentions a learning difference and being called a troubled kid. Keep the talk on how the narrator sees himself; don’t ask students to share their own labels.
Teacher
Read pages 1–8 aloud while students follow along. Use your voice to show the narrator’s sarcastic asides so students hear the tone.

Partner Reading & Discussion

Say these directions:
As I read, mark any sentence where the narrator tells you how he feels about himself. Put a star next to the one that surprises you most.
Ask:
Pause after page 3. The narrator warns readers to stop reading. What does that warning suggest about how he sees his own life?
Exemplar response
It suggests he thinks his life is dangerous or strange, and he doesn’t want normal kids to end up like him.
Ask:
Pause after page 6. The root mort means death. What does immortal mean, and why might it matter in this story?
Exemplar response
Immortal means not able to die. It might matter because it hints that the myths could be real in this book.
Pulse check (RL.6.1, L.6.4)
The narrator is on probation at school. Which meaning of probation fits best?
  • A
    A school award for good behavior
    Incorrect: A student may pick this if they read the word without the context of being in trouble.
  • B
    A trial period when behavior is watched before a final decision
    Correct: It fits a student who has been in trouble and is being given one more chance.
  • C
    A test given at the end of the school year
    Incorrect: A student may pick this because both happen at school; nothing in the text points to a test.
  • D
    A break from classes for a family trip
    Incorrect: A student may confuse it with time away from school; probation means staying and being watched.
Integrated ELD instructions
Purpose
Support students in naming the narrator’s feelings with precise words so they can make inferences from his comments.
Language focusfrustratedembarrassedwarnssuggests that
Say and ask
  • Before partners talk, have them point to the sentence they starred.
  • Recast: You said “he’s mad.” We can say “he sounds frustrated with how adults treat him.”
  • Frame: The narrator says ___. This suggests that he feels ___.
  • Frame: When he calls himself ___, I infer ___.
  • Invite students to rehearse their inference in a shared home language first, then in English.
Watch for and support if needed
  • If students retell events instead of inferring → Prompt: “What does that detail show about how he feels?”
  • Success: Students use “This suggests that” with one detail from pages 1–8.

Part B: Writing about the narrator’s fears RL.6.1RL.6.212 min

Say these directions:
Write three to four sentences that answer the question. Use one detail from today’s pages and the words “This suggests that.”
Ask:
How do Percy’s comments in the opening pages show his world values (and fears) about “normal” kids, and how might these opening pages be setting him up to become a hero or “problem” figure in a modern myth?
Exemplar response
Percy tells readers they should stop reading if they think they are like him. This suggests that he is afraid of being different and wishes he were a normal kid. He also jokes about the schools he has been kicked out of, which suggests that adults have already decided he is a problem. These pages could be setting him up to be a hero who proves everyone wrong.
Reflection (RL.6.1)
How sure are you that your detail supports your inference? Rate yourself from 1 to 5 using the Reflection routine.

4

Look Back

5 min

Quick Write

Say these directions:
In two sentences, predict what kind of hero Percy might become. Use one word from today’s vocabulary.
Ask:
What is one thing Percy fears, and how might that fear matter later in a myth-like story?
Exemplar response
Percy fears being a problem kid forever. In a myth the hero usually has to face what they fear, so he might have to prove he belongs.

Homework

Teacher
Have students take the novel home. Instruct students to:
  • Read and annotate Chapter 1, pp. 9–15. Mark two places where Percy’s tone changes.
  • Read and annotate the excerpt from “The Hare and the Lion.”
